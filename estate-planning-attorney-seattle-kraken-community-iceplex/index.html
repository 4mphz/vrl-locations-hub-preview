Wills, Trusts, and Estate Planning Services Offered

When people say "estate plan," they are usually talking about a small set of documents that work together. The practice here focuses on that core work: wills, trusts, and powers of attorney. Those three cover most of what a Washington resident needs in order to name decision-makers and direct where property goes.

It helps to see what each one does, because they solve different problems and are not interchangeable.

A will states who receives your property and who you want to serve as your personal representative. Without one, Washington's default rules decide distribution, and your family has no written instruction from you to follow.

A trust holds property under terms you set, managed by a trustee for the people you name. Trusts are used when you want control over timing and conditions rather than a single transfer, or when you want property handled outside a probate proceeding.

Powers of attorney name someone to act for you while you are alive but unable to act for yourself, whether for financial matters or health care decisions. This is the document that matters most in an incapacity situation, and it is the one people most often leave undone.

Most plans are some combination of these. A young family with a house and minor children has different priorities than a retired couple thinking about how their adult children will divide property. The documents are the same tools; the terms inside them are what get drafted to fit your situation.

Estate planning is a stated focus of this practice, and that matters in a practical way. It means the drafting work is not something squeezed in between unrelated matters. It also means the conversation is built around your circumstances rather than around a fill-in-the-blank template. The tradeoffs in this work are real: a trust gives you more control but requires more setup and ongoing attention than a will alone. A power of attorney gives someone meaningful authority over your affairs, which is exactly why the choice of person and the scope of that authority deserve thought rather than a quick signature.

The firm also practices family law. That combination is worth mentioning because the two areas overlap more often than people expect. A divorce changes who your beneficiaries should be, who holds your powers of attorney, and how community property is characterized. A remarriage raises questions about children from a prior relationship. If your family situation is changing, the estate planning documents you signed years ago may no longer say what you would want them to say.

If you are trying to decide what you need, that is a reasonable first conversation to have. You do not have to arrive knowing whether you want a will or a trust. The point of the initial discussion is to figure out which documents fit what you are trying to protect, and what each one will require of you.
