Wills, Trusts, and Estate Planning Documents Explained

When people say they need "a will," they usually mean something broader. Estate planning is the work of putting your decisions about property and authority into documents that hold up when someone has to rely on them. Our firm provides estate planning legal services, and that is the area we can speak to on this page.

It helps to understand what the pieces do, because the terms get used loosely. A will is a written direction about who receives your property after you die and who is responsible for carrying that out. A trust is a separate arrangement in which property is held and managed under terms you set, which can apply during your lifetime as well as after. A power of attorney is different from both: it grants authority to someone you name to act on your behalf while you are alive, typically if you become unable to handle financial or legal matters yourself.

Those distinctions matter because they answer different questions. A will does nothing until you die. A power of attorney does nothing after you die. If the concern that brought you here is a parent who is losing the ability to manage their own affairs, a will is not the document that addresses it. If the concern is who inherits the house, a power of attorney is not either. Part of a first conversation is sorting out which question you are trying to answer.

Washington has its own rules for how planning documents are executed and how estates are administered, which is why signing formalities and language choices are not clerical details. A document that says roughly the right thing but was not executed properly can create exactly the problem it was meant to prevent.

There are a few common reasons people get in touch about this work:

They have never had estate planning documents drafted and want a first set in place.

They signed documents years ago and circumstances have changed enough that the old language no longer reflects their wishes.

They want to name a specific person to act for them if they cannot act for themselves.

Someone in the family has died and they are trying to understand what happens next with the estate.
